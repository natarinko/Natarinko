# Information

## Introduction

**Information** is organized, interpreted, or structured data that conveys meaning and reduces uncertainty. It is one of the fundamental concepts in philosophy, communication, computer science, information science, mathematics, and many other disciplines. Information enables individuals and organizations to understand events, make decisions, solve problems, and communicate knowledge.

In everyday language, the terms *data*, *information*, and *knowledge* are often used interchangeably. However, in academic disciplines these concepts have distinct meanings. Data are raw observations or measurements. Information emerges when data are organized or interpreted in a meaningful context. Knowledge develops when information is understood, evaluated, and applied.

The rapid growth of digital technologies has transformed information into one of the world's most valuable resources. Modern societies depend on the continuous creation, storage, processing, transmission, and interpretation of information in nearly every field, including science, education, medicine, engineering, economics, and government.

---

## Definition

Although no single definition is universally accepted, information is commonly understood as meaningful data that can be used to increase understanding or support decision-making.

Different disciplines define information from different perspectives.

- In information science, information is meaningful content communicated between sources and recipients.
- In computer science, information often refers to processed data that can be stored, transmitted, or manipulated by computing systems.
- In communication theory, information is viewed as the content transmitted through communication channels.
- In philosophy, information is examined as a fundamental concept related to knowledge, meaning, truth, and representation.

Despite these differences, most definitions agree that information must possess meaning within a particular context.

---

## Etymology

The English word **information** originates from the Latin *informatio*, meaning "a concept," "an outline," or "instruction." It derives from the verb *informare*, meaning "to shape," "to form," or "to instruct."

During the Middle Ages, the word referred primarily to education and instruction. By the modern period, its meaning expanded to include facts, communication, and recorded knowledge. In the twentieth century, advances in mathematics, telecommunications, and computer science greatly broadened its technical significance.

## Information, Data, and Knowledge

Information is frequently confused with data and knowledge, yet these concepts describe different stages in the organization of understanding.

### Data

Data consist of raw observations, symbols, numbers, measurements, or facts that have not yet been interpreted.

Examples include:

- temperatures recorded every hour;
- examination scores;
- satellite measurements;
- individual words without context.

By themselves, data may have little practical meaning.

### Information

Information results when data are organized, interpreted, classified, or presented within a meaningful context.

For example, a list of temperatures becomes information when it is presented as a weather report showing daily trends.

Information answers questions such as:

- What happened?
- When did it happen?
- Where did it happen?

### Knowledge

Knowledge develops when information is combined with understanding, reasoning, experience, and critical evaluation.

Knowledge enables prediction, explanation, and decision-making.

For example, a physician combines medical information with education and clinical experience to diagnose disease and recommend treatment.

## Characteristics of Information

Information generally possesses several important characteristics.

### Meaning

Information must communicate meaning to its intended audience.

### Accuracy

Accurate information correctly represents reality and minimizes error.

### Relevance

Information should be appropriate for the purpose or decision it supports.

### Timeliness

Information often loses value if it is outdated or delayed.

### Completeness

Incomplete information may lead to misunderstanding or poor decisions.

### Reliability

Reliable information originates from trustworthy sources and can be verified.

### Accessibility

Information should be available to those who legitimately need it while remaining appropriately protected when confidentiality is required.

## Importance

Information plays an essential role in modern civilization.

It supports:

- scientific discovery;
- education;
- healthcare;
- engineering;
- public administration;
- journalism;
- economic activity;
- technological innovation;
- environmental management;
- international cooperation.

The ability to collect, organize, evaluate, and communicate information has become one of the defining characteristics of contemporary society.## Classification of Information

Information can be classified in many ways depending on its source, purpose, and form.

### Qualitative and Quantitative Information

**Qualitative information** describes qualities, characteristics, or observations that cannot easily be expressed numerically.

Examples include:

- colors;
- opinions;
- descriptions of events;
- interview responses.

**Quantitative information** consists of measurable values expressed numerically.

Examples include:

- population statistics;
- temperatures;
- financial reports;
- scientific measurements.

---

### Primary and Secondary Information

**Primary information** is obtained directly from original observations, experiments, surveys, or firsthand accounts.

**Secondary information** is derived from the analysis, interpretation, or summarization of primary sources.

Academic research often relies on both forms of information.

---

### Public and Private Information

**Public information** is intended to be openly accessible.

Examples include:

- published scientific papers;
- government reports;
- public statistics.

**Private information** is restricted because of legal, ethical, or personal considerations.

Examples include:

- medical records;
- financial accounts;
- confidential business documents.

## Information Theory

Modern information theory was established in 1948 by the American mathematician Claude Shannon.

Shannon developed a mathematical framework for measuring information and analyzing communication systems. His work introduced concepts such as information entropy, channel capacity, encoding, and noise.

Information theory focuses on the efficient transmission and storage of information rather than its semantic meaning. It has become fundamental to digital communication, telecommunications, computer networks, and data compression.

Today, information theory influences fields including:

- computer science;
- telecommunications;
- cryptography;
- artificial intelligence;
- signal processing;
- data science.

---

## Information in Computing

Computers represent information digitally using binary digits, commonly known as **bits**.

All forms of digital content—including text, images, audio, and video—are ultimately encoded as sequences of binary values.

Modern computer systems continuously perform operations such as:

- collecting information;
- storing information;
- processing information;
- transmitting information;
- protecting information.

The growth of cloud computing, high-speed networks, and artificial intelligence has dramatically increased the volume of information processed worldwide.

---

## Information in Biology

Living organisms also depend on information.

Genetic information is encoded within DNA and transmitted from one generation to the next. Cells continuously process chemical signals that regulate growth, development, and reproduction.

Animals receive information through sensory systems such as vision, hearing, smell, taste, and touch. The nervous system interprets these signals, enabling organisms to respond to their environment.

Because of these processes, many scientists consider biological systems to be information-processing systems.

## Information and Communication

Information gains value when it can be communicated effectively.

Communication involves:

- a sender;
- a message;
- a communication channel;
- a receiver.

During transmission, information may be affected by interference, misunderstanding, or loss. Effective communication therefore depends on clarity, accuracy, and appropriate context.

Modern communication technologies—including the Internet, satellite systems, and mobile networks—allow information to be exchanged globally within seconds.## Information in Society

Information is one of the most valuable resources in modern society. Governments, businesses, educational institutions, scientific organizations, and individuals rely on information to make decisions and achieve their objectives.

Access to accurate and timely information contributes to economic development, public health, scientific progress, and democratic governance. Conversely, the absence of reliable information can lead to poor decisions, inefficiency, and social instability.

The expansion of digital technologies has transformed information into a global resource that can be created, shared, and accessed almost instantly.

## Information Quality

The usefulness of information depends on its quality.

Important dimensions of information quality include:

- **Accuracy** — correctly represents reality.
- **Completeness** — contains all necessary details.
- **Consistency** — does not contain contradictions.
- **Reliability** — comes from trustworthy sources.
- **Timeliness** — is sufficiently up to date.
- **Relevance** — addresses the needs of its users.
- **Accessibility** — can be obtained and understood by its intended audience.

Poor-quality information may result in misunderstanding, financial loss, or incorrect decisions.

## The Information Age

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries are often described as the **Information Age** because of the rapid growth of digital communication, computing, and global networks.

Characteristics of the Information Age include:

- widespread Internet access;
- digital communication;
- cloud computing;
- artificial intelligence;
- big data;
- social media;
- online education;
- electronic commerce.

These developments have significantly increased both the availability of information and the speed at which it can be exchanged.

## Misinformation and Disinformation

Not all information is accurate.

**Misinformation** is false or inaccurate information shared without the intention to deceive.

**Disinformation** is deliberately false information created or distributed to mislead people.

Because digital communication enables information to spread rapidly, evaluating the credibility of sources has become an increasingly important skill.

Critical thinking, fact-checking, and the use of reliable references are essential for distinguishing trustworthy information from unreliable or misleading content.

The creation and use of information involve important ethical questions.

- transparency of public information;
- cybersecurity;
